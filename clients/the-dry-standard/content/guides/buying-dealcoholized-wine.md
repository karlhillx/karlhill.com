Most disappointing NA wine is not bad because the alcohol is gone. It is bad because the producer started with indifferent wine, stripped it hot, then dosed it with sweetness to hide the thinness.

## Start with the grape, not the zero

If you would not drink the estate's full-strength bottle, do not expect the dealcoholized nephew to be a revelation. Leitz, Giesen, and a handful of European houses at least begin with a real varietal program.

## Ask how the alcohol left

Vacuum distillation is the method you will see most often on wines that bother to explain themselves. Spinning-cone columns show up in trade sheets. Reverse osmosis exists; it is less commonly advertised on consumer pages. If the only word on the site is "removed," keep walking.

## Taste for acid, not for body

Ethanol gives wine weight. After it is gone, acidity and bitterness do the structural work. A dealcoholized Riesling with real acid can handle 30-plus grams of residual sugar and still feel like wine. A dealcoholized Chardonnay with no acid just tastes like apple juice that went to finishing school.

## Expect the bubbles to be added

Very few NA sparkling wines complete a traditional second fermentation and then get dealcoholized. More often a still wine is stripped and carbonated. That can be pleasant. It is not grower Champagne, and a review that pretends otherwise is selling something.

## Shop like a grown-up

US specialty retailers — Boisson, The Zero Proof, a competent wine shop — will usually beat a grocery "NA" endcap. Check the vintage policy: many of these wines are NV, and freshness still matters once the alcohol is gone.
